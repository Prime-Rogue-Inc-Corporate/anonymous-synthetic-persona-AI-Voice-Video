WHITLOW: Two weeks ago, the voters threw out the majority. This chamber is about to change hands. A whole lot of new members are coming in January, and every one of them thinks they're going to be different.
WHITLOW: Some of them will be. Most of them, within a year, will discover the meeting. You know the one. The one you have the moment someone hands you a pen.
WHITLOW: I want to say to the new members: welcome. Read the bill. Vote how you said you would. When leadership tells you it's a team vote, ask what the team is.
WHITLOW: And when they tell you you'll lose your committee seats, tell them I said hello. Tell them I lost mine and I'm still here, talking.
TEAGUE: Will the gentleman yield so that the gentleman can stop talking?
WHITLOW: No, but I appreciate the creativity. That's the most original idea leadership has had all year.
CHAIR: [gavel] Members will refrain from personalities.
WHITLOW: Madam Speaker, I withdraw the compliment.
WHITLOW: Madam Speaker, before I finish, I want to read some of my mail. People think members of Congress don't read their mail. Most of us don't. I do. And since I'm leaving, I can finally answer some of it out loud.
WHITLOW: I've removed the names. These are real kinds of letters. Every member gets them. Most members answer them with a form letter that says, "Thank you for sharing your views."
WHITLOW: Letter number one. "Dear Congressman. Why did you vote against the spending bill? Don't you want the government to stay open?"
WHITLOW: Dear constituent. I want the government to stay open. I also want it to stay solvent. Those used to be the same goal. I voted against a bill that kept the lights on by borrowing against your grandchildren's lights. Thank you for sharing your views.
WHITLOW: Letter number two. "Dear Congressman. You are the only one who votes no on everything. Are you even trying to get anything done?"
WHITLOW: Dear constituent. I have voted yes on plenty of things. I voted yes on the war powers resolutions. I voted yes on releasing the files. I voted yes on requiring a warrant before the government reads your messages. Those all lost. So I get credit for voting no, and none for voting yes. That's how the scoreboard works here. Thank you for sharing your views.
WHITLOW: Letter number three. "Dear Congressman. My son is deployed. Can you tell me what the mission is and when it ends?"
WHITLOW: Madam Speaker, I've gotten that letter more than once this year. And I want to tell you, I couldn't answer it. Not because it's classified. Because Congress never asked.
WHITLOW: That mother wrote to the one branch of government the Constitution says is supposed to decide whether her son goes to war. And that branch didn't have an answer, because that branch never voted.
WHITLOW: I wrote back and told her the truth. I told her I didn't know, and I told her I was ashamed that I didn't know, and I told her I'd keep asking for a vote until my last day. This is my last month. I'm still asking.
WHITLOW: Letter number four. This one's shorter. "Dear Congressman. Who even are you?"
WHITLOW: Dear constituent. As of January, nobody. Thank you for sharing your views.
WHITLOW: Letter number five. "Dear Congressman. I'm sorry you lost. I didn't vote for you, but I always knew where you stood."
WHITLOW: Madam Speaker, that's the best review I ever got. I want it on my headstone. Not the part about losing. The part about knowing where I stood.
WHITLOW: Because that's all I ever wanted the people back home to be able to say. Not that they agreed with me. That they knew where I stood. That I read the bill and voted the way I said I would and came home and told them why.
WHITLOW: That shouldn't be rare in this building, Madam Speaker. It should be the minimum. It should be the thing they check at the door, like the metal detectors.
TEAGUE: Will the gentleman yield?
WHITLOW: For what purpose?
TEAGUE: The deputy whip would also like to know who the gentleman is.
WHITLOW: The gentleman has served with me for six years.

WHITLOW: Now. I promised I'd come back to the team thing.
WHITLOW: For fourteen years, Madam Speaker, I've been told I'm not a team player.
WHITLOW: I've been told it by my own leadership. I've been told it by the other side's leadership. I've been told it on television. I was told it in a very expensive primary by a very good photograph.
WHITLOW: And I want to say, here, on the record, in front of the Chair and the Clerk and the clock: that's correct.
WHITLOW: I'm not a team player.
WHITLOW: Because I looked at the team. The team has spent us forty trillion dollars into the hole. The team went to war without a vote. The team voted to read your mail. The team kept the files in the drawer. The team took a quarter of the year off and named a dinosaur.
WHITLOW: The team is losing, Madam Speaker. The team has been losing for a long time. It just keeps getting re-elected.
WHITLOW: I'm not on it.
WHITLOW: I'll tell you what team I'm on. I'm on the team of the people back home who sent me here with a very simple instruction. Read the bill. Vote how you said you would. Come home and tell us the truth.
WHITLOW: That team is not very popular in this building. It doesn't have a whip. It doesn't have a gift shop. It doesn't have a PAC.
WHITLOW: But it's got a lot of members, Madam Speaker. More than you'd think. They just aren't in this room. They're at work. They have to be. Somebody has to pay the interest.
WHITLOW: I want to tell you about a man in my district. I'm not going to use his name, because he'd hate that. He runs a small shop. Eleven employees. He's been in business thirty years.
WHITLOW: He told me once, "Clay, if I ran my shop like you all run Congress, I'd have been out of business in a week, and my employees would have been right to leave."
WHITLOW: I said, "Well, what would you do?"
WHITLOW: He said, "I'd show up. I'd read the invoices before I signed them. And I wouldn't spend money I didn't have on things I didn't need to impress people I don't like."
WHITLOW: Madam Speaker, that man should be the Speaker of the House. He won't be, because he has a job.

WHITLOW: So let me wrap up.
WHITLOW: In the next five weeks, Madam Speaker, here's what's going to happen. I want the folks at home to keep this list and check it against what they see.
WHITLOW: Number one. On or around December eleventh, there will be a crisis. The crisis will have been on the calendar since September. Everyone will act surprised.
WHITLOW: Number two. There will be a giant bill. It will arrive late at night. It will be called urgent. It will be very long. Almost nobody will read it.
WHITLOW: Number three. Somewhere in that bill there will be something that has nothing to do with keeping the government open. A tax break, a pet project, a favor. Something somebody couldn't pass on its own, so they attached it to something nobody can vote against.
WHITLOW: Number four. The war will not get a vote.
WHITLOW: Number five. The files will not get a vote, unless a handful more members find a working pen. The pens work. I keep saying it. I'll say it again. The pens work.
WHITLOW: Number six. Both parties will go on television and blame the other one for all of it. And both of them will be right.
WHITLOW: Number seven. Somebody will rename a post office.
WHITLOW: And then on January third, the new Congress will be sworn in. The gavels will change hands. The jerseys will change colors. And everybody in this building will start walking in circles again, just from the other direction.
WHITLOW: I won't be here for it, Madam Speaker. I'm going home in January. I'm going back to the river and the shop and the tractor that lost to a photograph.
WHITLOW: And I'd encourage my colleagues to try it sometime. Go home. Not for a district work period. For real.
WHITLOW: Talk to the man with eleven employees. Talk to the farmer who plants in the spring. Talk to the family paying seven dollars for gas. Ask them what they think of the job you're doing.
WHITLOW: And on the flight back, read the bill. It's long. The flight is short. You'll have to start early.
