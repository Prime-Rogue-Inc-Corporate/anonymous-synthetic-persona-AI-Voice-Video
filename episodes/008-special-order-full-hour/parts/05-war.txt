WHITLOW: Now I want to be serious for a few minutes, Madam Speaker. Because there's a thing this Congress didn't do that matters more than all the rest.
WHITLOW: Article One, Section Eight of the Constitution says Congress shall have the power to declare war.
WHITLOW: Not the President. Not the Secretary of War. Not a cable news panel. Congress. Us. The people in these chairs. The empty chairs, mostly, but still.
WHITLOW: On February twenty-eighth of this year, the United States went to war with Iran.
WHITLOW: Congress did not declare that war. Congress did not authorize that war. Congress was, at best, informed of that war.
WHITLOW: In nineteen seventy-three, after Vietnam, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over a President's veto. It says that when troops are sent into hostilities without authorization, the clock starts. Sixty days, with a possible thirty more.
WHITLOW: That clock ran out in the spring. Nobody stopped it. Nobody even looked at it. It's like the clock on that wall, except nobody in this building has ever cared what that one says.
WHITLOW: In March, I voted for a war powers resolution. It failed, two hundred twelve to two hundred nineteen.
WHITLOW: In May, I voted for another one. It failed on a tie. Two hundred twelve to two hundred twelve.
WHITLOW: Madam Speaker, the question of whether this country is at war came down to a tie vote in a half-empty Congress, and the tie went to the war.
WHITLOW: Since then, American service members have died in that war. Their families deserve a Congress that had the courage to vote on it. Not one way or the other. Just vote.
WHITLOW: If you believe in this war, stand up and authorize it. Put your name on it. If you don't, stand up and end it. Put your name on that.
WHITLOW: What you don't get to do is hide in the cloakroom and let the clock run out, and then go home and give a speech about supporting the troops.
WHITLOW: I want to be fair here, because both parties love to be outraged about this when the other side holds the White House.
WHITLOW: When the President is from their party, the war powers are a technicality. When the President is from the other party, the war powers are sacred. The Constitution doesn't change depending on who's in the Oval Office. Only the speeches do.
WHITLOW: We found time to vote on the dinosaur, Madam Speaker. We never found time to vote on the war.
WHITLOW: We declared a dinosaur. We didn't declare the war.
CHAIR: The gentleman has twenty-four minutes remaining.
WHITLOW: Thank you, Madam Speaker. That's twenty-four more minutes than the war got.
