WHITLOW: There is a law that sets a limit on how much the government can borrow. It's called the debt limit. Right now that limit is forty-one point one trillion dollars.
WHITLOW: We are at forty trillion. So we have about a trillion dollars of room left. At the rate we're going, that's not a cushion, Madam Speaker. That's a speed bump.
WHITLOW: Budget experts estimate we'll hit the limit sometime between late winter and the middle of next summer. Which means the brand-new Congress, a few months after it's sworn in, will walk straight into it.
WHITLOW: And here's how that will go. I can tell you, because I've watched it go the same way every time.
WHITLOW: First, the party that's out of the White House will discover that it cares deeply about the debt. It will hold press conferences. It will bring charts. The charts will be very large.
WHITLOW: Then the party in the White House will say that refusing to raise the limit is hostage-taking, and that it's irresponsible to threaten the full faith and credit of the United States.
WHITLOW: Then, a few years later, they'll switch sides and give each other's speeches. Word for word. I've checked. Some of them have kept the same charts.
WHITLOW: And at the last possible moment, at two in the morning, they will raise the limit. Usually with a big spending deal attached. And everyone will declare victory, and the debt will keep climbing, and in two years we'll do it again.
WHITLOW: Madam Speaker, the debt limit has been raised or suspended dozens and dozens of times in my lifetime. It is the only limit in Washington that everyone agrees is a limit, and that nobody has ever let stop them.
WHITLOW: It's like a diet where the rule is you can eat anything you want, as long as you announce it first.
