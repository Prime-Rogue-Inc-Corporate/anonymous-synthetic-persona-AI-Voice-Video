WHITLOW: Madam Speaker, I want to explain to the folks at home how a bill really becomes a law. Because what they taught you in school was very nice, and it was also fiction.
WHITLOW: In school, they tell you a member has an idea. The member writes a bill. It goes to committee. The committee debates it. It comes to the floor. Everyone debates it. Everyone votes. It goes to the other body. They debate it. The President signs it.
WHITLOW: Here's how it actually works.
WHITLOW: Step one: a lobbyist has an idea.
WHITLOW: Step two: the idea is written into a bill by a staffer who used to work for the lobbyist and will work for the lobbyist again in about eighteen months.
WHITLOW: Step three: the bill goes to the Rules Committee. The Rules Committee is a small room upstairs where the real decisions are made. It decides what you're allowed to vote on and what you're not.
WHITLOW: Most of the time, the answer is: you can vote yes or no on the whole thing, and you can't change a word of it. That's called a closed rule. It's called that because your mouth is supposed to stay closed.
WHITLOW: Step four: the bill comes to the floor. There's an hour of debate. The debate is mostly members reading speeches written by staff to a room with nobody in it, for clips they'll post later.
WHITLOW: I want to acknowledge, Madam Speaker, that I'm currently doing that. But I wrote mine myself. You can tell because it's too long.
CHAIR: The Chair agrees.
WHITLOW: Thank you, Madam Speaker. That's the first bipartisan agreement I've had all year.
WHITLOW: Step five: the vote. Members run in from the cloakroom, look up at the board to see how their party is voting, and vote the same way. Some of them don't know what the bill is. Some of them don't know which bill it is.
WHITLOW: I once watched a member vote, turn to the person next to him, and ask what he'd just voted for. The other member said, "I think it was the post office." It wasn't. It was the post office the week before.
WHITLOW: Step six: the post office.
WHITLOW: I want to talk about the post offices, Madam Speaker. This House renames a lot of post offices. It's one of our most reliable products.
WHITLOW: Somewhere in my district there's a post office that I'm fairly sure has been renamed four times. The mail still comes late under every name.
WHITLOW: It's the perfect bill. Nobody objects to it. It costs nothing. It does nothing. And every member gets to send a press release saying they did something.
WHITLOW: If we could run the whole government the way we run the post office naming bills, we'd be the most productive Congress in history. We would also, technically, have accomplished nothing.
WHITLOW: Which, if you look at the record, we did anyway. We just took longer.

WHITLOW: Which post office?
TEAGUE: Any post office.
WHITLOW: No.
